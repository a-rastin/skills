# Scientific writing, reporting, and citation audit

## 1. Audit the argument and structure

Trace the logic from knowledge gap to question, design, result, and conclusion. Flag:

- objectives that change across abstract, introduction, and methods;
- hypotheses introduced only after results;
- background material that does not motivate the study question;
- methods or results presented in a nonreproducible order;
- discussion claims unsupported by a reported analysis;
- limitations that are generic, minimized, or disconnected from likely bias;
- conclusions that do not answer the stated question or ignore uncertainty.

Require each paragraph to have one scientific function and a clear topic sentence. Remove repetition, vague transitions, and rhetorical certainty unsupported by evidence. Preserve necessary nuance and the author’s technical meaning.

## 2. Calibrate claims and causal language

Match verbs to evidence:

- Use association language when the design does not identify a causal contrast.
- Reserve causal language for designs and assumptions that support it, with the target estimand clear.
- Distinguish “no statistically significant evidence” from “no effect,” “equivalent,” or “safe.”
- Distinguish a larger estimate from a statistically supported interaction or between-group difference.
- Distinguish a surrogate, intermediate, biomarker, or model prediction from a patient-important or observed outcome.
- Avoid “predictor” as a causal term and clarify whether it means a model input, prognostic factor, or temporal forecast.
- Report uncertainty, bias, multiplicity, and exploratory status wherever central claims appear, including the abstract.

Check spin: selective focus on favorable secondary outcomes, within-group significance, relative rather than absolute effects, subgroup narratives without interaction evidence, or recommendations exceeding the population and follow-up.

## 3. Enforce consistent terminology and definitions

Create a terminology ledger for population labels, group names, exposures, interventions, outcomes, instruments, time points, analysis sets, abbreviations, and effect measures.

Flag silent changes such as:

- incidence versus prevalence;
- risk, rate, odds, probability, and hazard;
- accuracy, discrimination, calibration, and validation;
- sex and gender without a defined measurement;
- subject, participant, patient, sample, and observation used interchangeably;
- technical and biological replicates conflated;
- SD, SE, CI, IQR, credible interval, and prediction interval mislabeled;
- adjusted and unadjusted estimates described as the same result.

Define specialized terms once, use standard nomenclature, and include units and scale direction.

## 4. Audit numerical and visual reporting

Require tables and figures to stand alone without contradicting the text. Check titles, legends, denominators, units, analysis population, missing values, uncertainty type, reference category, adjustment set, multiplicity, and abbreviations.

- Use precision justified by measurement and analysis; avoid excess decimals and inconsistent rounding.
- Report exact p-values when appropriate, while using bounded notation for extremely small values rather than `p = 0.000`.
- Pair estimates with compatible uncertainty intervals and include absolute effects when clinically meaningful.
- Distinguish SD from SE and variability from uncertainty.
- Avoid bar charts that hide distributions when dot, box, violin, interval, or trajectory displays better represent the data.
- Show axes, scales, transformations, truncation, and uncertainty honestly. Flag distorted axes, dual axes, area/volume encodings, or omitted zero only when they materially alter interpretation.
- Ensure colors, markers, and labels remain interpretable for grayscale and common color-vision differences.
- Reconcile every highlighted value with the source table or analysis.

Do not silently correct a number. Record the discrepancy and how the corrected value was derived.

## 5. Require reproducibility and transparency

Check whether another qualified analyst could reconstruct:

- recruitment, eligibility, sampling, allocation, and exclusions;
- variable definitions, instruments, scoring, units, transformations, and cut points;
- primary/secondary outcomes, time points, estimand, and analysis population;
- complete model specification, interactions, nonlinearities, weights, correlations/random effects, and variance estimators;
- missing-data handling, multiplicity, diagnostics, sensitivity analyses, and software/version;
- sample-size calculation and all its assumptions;
- data/code/material availability and any access restrictions;
- registration, protocol, analysis-plan, amendment, and repository identifiers with dates.

Distinguish “not reported” from “not done.” Recommend the exact detail or artifact needed. Do not demand public release of identifiable or restricted data; controlled access, synthetic examples, metadata, code, or independent reanalysis may be appropriate.

## 6. Check the applicable reporting guideline

Identify the study design first, then use the current official checklist and extensions when external verification is available. Common mappings include:

- randomized trial: CONSORT and relevant extensions;
- observational study: STROBE;
- systematic review/meta-analysis: PRISMA and relevant extensions;
- diagnostic accuracy: STARD;
- prediction model: TRIPOD and relevant extensions;
- animal/preclinical study: ARRIVE;
- economic evaluation: CHEERS;
- case report: CARE;
- qualitative study: an appropriate guideline such as SRQR or COREQ.

Do not equate checklist completion with methodological validity. Report missing items that affect interpretation or reproducibility, not merely item numbers. Verify the guideline version and official wording before claiming compliance.

## 7. Verify references and citations

Audit both support and bibliographic integrity:

- Check that every central factual, methodological, and comparative claim has an appropriate source.
- Prefer the original study, primary methodological paper, official guideline, registry record, data source, or authoritative manual over a secondary summary when the exact claim matters.
- Verify author, title, journal/source, year, volume/pages or article number, DOI/identifier, and link before citing.
- Check whether a cited source actually supports the sentence, population, direction, and magnitude claimed.
- Identify citation distortion, overgeneralization from a narrow study, secondary citation presented as primary, and inappropriate self-citation clusters.
- Check for corrections, expressions of concern, retractions, superseded guidelines, duplicate publications, and overlapping cohorts when current external verification is authorized.
- Distinguish absence of a citation from a false citation and a source disagreement from a manuscript misquotation.

Never fabricate or complete a partial reference from memory. Mark it “unverified” and request the bibliography or verify it externally. Quote sparingly and accurately; paraphrase without changing the source’s qualification.

## 8. Revise precisely

For each writing issue, provide:

1. exact location and problematic phrase or claim;
2. the scientific problem, not merely a style preference;
3. a replacement that preserves the justified meaning;
4. any fact, analysis, or citation that must be verified before adoption.

Prioritize validity and interpretability over cosmetic editing. Do not polish language in a way that conceals uncertainty, design limitations, missing analyses, or unresolved numerical conflicts. When meaning is ambiguous, offer a conditional revision rather than selecting an interpretation without evidence.
